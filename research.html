Job Market Paper

Engineering Germany’s Industrial Success

Professional Societies and the Social Dynamics of Innovation

Germany was among the few countries that reached the global technological frontier, rising from an industrial laggard in 1850 to a world leader in chemistry and electrical machinery by 1913. What role did the organization of its engineers play in this catch-up? I study the German Engineering Society, whose local chapters held regular meetings at which engineers and owners of different firms discussed technical papers and visited plants. Exploiting the staggered founding of chapters between 1880 and 1912 in a difference-in-differences design, I find that the annual number of high-value patents in a chapter's region rose by 4.8 percent after it opened. Pre-trends are flat, and the effect is robust to controlling for prior industrialization. The increase was concentrated in industrial fields in which Society members worked. After an opening, high-value patents became more similar to earlier patents from their own district, consistent with knowledge exchange among local engineers. A century later, counties near the chapter towns still patent more.

Drained Natural Assets

Pricing the Ecosystem Services of U.S. Peatlands

with Nils Lieber

Drained peatlands emit carbon dioxide until they are rewetted. We estimate what it costs to conserve each 30 m cell of peatland in the conterminous United States. For every cell, we combine USDA Forest Service estimates of the avoidable emissions with land values and with the flood damages that wetlands prevent. With a 25-year contract and a 4 percent discount rate, 79 percent of U.S. peatland can be conserved for $50 per tonne of CO2 or less. This peatland accounts for 93 percent of the avoidable emissions. For the cheapest 45 percent, the avoided flood damages alone exceed the cost. Conserving peatland is thus cheap but small abatement: it would avoid about half a percent of U.S. greenhouse gas emissions.

Agricultural Roots of Industrial Success

How German Viticulture Shaped “Hidden Champions”

This paper investigates whether viticulture contributed to the emergence of Germany’s Hidden Champions, little-known medium-sized firms leading world markets in technical niches. Existing explanations emphasize national institutions, yet these firms cluster in particular regions. I link the 1,691 Hidden Champions in Venohr’s database to Germany’s 10,785 municipalities and use the Huglin Index, a climate-based measure of the suitability for wine growing, as an instrument for viticulture. With state fixed effects and historical and geographic controls, viticulture raises the log number of Hidden Champions in a municipality by 0.32. The estimate is similar for the wine-growing areas around 1900. Municipalities with viticulture are also more densely populated and have a higher employment density today, and around 1900 they had more members of the German engineers’ association. These results are consistent with the hypothesis that viticulture raised the technical skills of the local workforce, although the data do not allow a direct test. They suggest that the crops a region grew before industrialization can shape where specialized manufacturing firms are located more than a century later.
