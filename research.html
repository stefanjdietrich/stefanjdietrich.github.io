Engineering Germany’s Industrial Success

Engineering Societies, Patents, and the 19th-Century Catch-Up

Local chapters of the German Engineering Society (Verein Deutscher Ingenieure, VDI), founded in a staggered fashion between 1877 and 1918, raised annual high-value patenting in their districts by about four percent. I estimate this in a Callaway–Sant’Anna design on the Streb–Baten high-value patent corpus, exploiting the staggered opening of 22 chapters; pre-trends are flat, and patent classes outside the Society’s technical scope show a null. The response operates through strengthening existing capacity and through practicing owner-engineers rather than credentialed graduates. The professional organization of practical knowledge emerges as a channel for innovation capacity that complements formal education and patent rights, coordinating Germany’s late-19th-century catch-up in chemistry, electrical machinery, and precision instruments.

Drained Natural Assets

Pricing the Ecosystem Services of U.S. Peatlands

with Nils Lieber

We price two ecosystem services of U.S. peatlands at parcel scale: carbon and flood mitigation. Overlaying a new 30 m USDA Forest Service peatland flux raster on the Nolte (2020) land-value raster and the Taylor and Druckenmiller (2022) wetland flood-mitigation raster, we construct the first parcel-level marginal abatement cost curve for peatland conservation. At a 20-year horizon and a 4 percent discount rate, 79 percent of U.S. peatland is conservable at or below $50/tCO2, securing about 28.6 MtCO2 per year of avoided emissions for roughly $14 billion in present value while displacing less than two percent of U.S. corn output. Within-county dispersion in abatement cost rivals across-county dispersion, making spatial targeting rather than uniform per-hectare payments the binding policy lever; netting out roughly $15 billion in flood-mitigation co-benefits, more than 140,000 ha clear the welfare threshold at a zero carbon price.

Agricultural Roots of Industrial Skills

How German Viticulture Shaped “Hidden Champions”

Did historical wine cultivation leave a skill base that still shows up in Germany’s Hidden Champions — little-known world-market leaders in niche industrial products? Instrumenting municipal viticulture with the Huglin index, a growing-season temperature measure of climatic suitability for vines, I find that viticulture raises the number of Hidden Champions in a municipality by 31 percent. The candidate mechanism is human capital: wine cultivation demanded a broader, more technically diverse skill set than other crops, feeding into the technically oriented Mittelstand firms that became Hidden Champions. Work in progress; instrument validity tests (first stage, crop placebos, exclusion threats) are ongoing.
